🌙 Supplier Whisperer

An agent that works the night shift of a supplier negotiation. While a hardware engineer in the US sleeps, it answers overseas suppliers' technical questions from the product spec, sends a holding reply on anything that would commit the company, and stops prompt injection and supplier fraud before any AI model reads them.

It is not an email summarizer. It takes actions inside an ongoing supplier conversation (answering, holding, escalating, blocking) and a deterministic trust layer, not the model, decides what it may say.

2. Motivation: who has the problem

The person. For five years I took hardware products from prototype to mass production with contract manufacturers and part suppliers in China. The work runs on email: design-for-manufacturing (DFM) questions, tolerance clarifications, change proposals, quotes. Shenzhen is 12 hours ahead of US Eastern now and 13 hours after Nov 1 (China is UTC+8 with no daylight saving), so the supplier's working day is my night. [China Standard Time]

How it's handled today: two bad options.

  1. Sacrifice sleep. Calls at 7 am or 9 pm to overlap with the factory's day.
  2. Wait. Answer the next morning, so every clarification costs one full response cycle, about 24 hours. Practitioners describe quotes taking 24–48 h and each clarification costing "a full response cycle" [Hardware Secrets; practitioner evidence, not research-grade]. A DFM back-and-forth has several such cycles.

3. What is new here

Many products touch email and procurement. None of them does commitment-aware, spec-grounded replies on behalf of an engineer before the purchase order.

CategoryNamed productsWhy they don't solve this
Email infrastructure for agentsAgentMail (YC S25, $6M seed) [TechCrunch] Inboxes, allowlists, and "Agent Armor" (inbound injection scan) decide who an agent talks to and whether to hold mail [docs]. We found no check on what an outgoing reply commits you to, and it has no product knowledge. It's the layer we build on, not a competitor.
Procurement agentsDidero ($30M A) [SiliconANGLE], Lio ($30M A) [TechCrunch], Mandel AI, Lumari [YC], Kavida (QAD), Pactum, Keelvar, Fairmarkit, Zip, Levelpath Built for procurement teams after the PO: RFQs, PO follow-ups, ERP sync, negotiation of indirect spend, sold through enterprise sales cycles. They don't handle the engineer's pre-PO DFM conversation grounded in a product spec.
Hardware purchasingWaybill (YC S26) [YC] Parts purchasing for hardware teams (quotes, payment, customs). The closest threat, but it's purchasing, not engineering Q&A.
General email AIGmail Gemini, Copilot in Outlook, Superhuman, Fyxer, Lindy, ChatGPT They draft or summarize for a human to send. Lindy can send on its own, but approval is all-or-nothing per action: it can't tell "answers a draft angle" from "agrees to a price", and it doesn't read product docs.

What we do differently, and why it matters

  1. Commitment-aware outbound control. Every draft passes 11 deterministic checks (prices, quantities, dates, commitment language, links, attachments, confidential terms, recipients…) before it can leave. AgentMail decides who and whether; we decide what.
  2. Grounded in the spec, with data classes. Answers come only from [SHAREABLE] spec sections. [CONFIDENTIAL] sections (target cost, launch date, codename) never reach a model. Every number in an answer must exist in the spec, or the reply is not auto-sent.
  3. A supply-chain workflow, not a chatbot. Night/day autonomy, a risk-tolerance slider, holding replies, supplier-specific threats (lookalike domains, bank-change fraud), thread memory, a morning brief, and approval by email from a phone.
  4. Trust is the product. Orchestration is code; models can only make outcomes stricter; any error fails closed. In this domain, one wrong "yes" to a price can cost someone their job.
Stress test. "AgentMail or a funded procurement startup will add this." Partly fair: generic trust (who may email, injection scanning) is commoditized, so we use it as layer 0 rather than rebuild it. The durable part is domain-specific content policy plus eval data from real supplier threads (§7–8), and the engineer-facing wedge before the PO, where startups often have no ERP or procurement team. Positioning: "built on AgentMail, for hardware engineers", not "versus AgentMail".

4. The agents

Four gpt-6-luna agents do the language work. Plain Python does everything that has consequences: gates, ordering, decisions, recipients, sending. No model has a tool that sends, approves, attaches files or picks recipients.

ManagerTriage: intent, numbered questions, commitment topics (price, quantity, date, PO, payment, drawing, engineering change, identity), suspicious-instruction flagVisible email text only, wrapped in <supplier_email> tags marking it as untrusted data; thread memoryJSON

Why gpt-6-luna. It is the course model ($0.10 input / $0.01 cached input / $0.50 output per 1M tokens, lecture 11). The tasks are structured extraction and short drafting, which don't need a frontier model, and the safety doesn't depend on the model being smart: the code checks everything it says. Why four small agents instead of one. Each sees the minimum it needs: the reviewer never sees the supplier's text, the context agent never sees confidential sections, and the email agent never sees unapproved answers.

5. Tokenomics

Method. scripts/measure_tokens.py ran the 9-email inbox live three times at the default reasoning effort and three times at REASONING_EFFORT=low, each on a fresh database (Night mode, Medium risk). Token counts come from the Responses API usage field of every call; cost uses the lecture-11 formula. Raw data: docs/tokenomics.json.

A measurement trap we found. Repeated live runs first showed identical token counts. The cause was Portkey's response cache: on repeat runs every call returned x-portkey-cache-status: HIT, the stored answer with its original token counts. The measurement therefore sends x-portkey-cache-force-refresh on every call (the app's new PORTKEY_CACHE=refresh setting) and logs the header: REFRESH on all 132 measured calls. In normal use the cache is a free cost cut for identical emails, so it stays on by default.

6. Safety

The design principle: models may only make an outcome stricter. Topic detection is the union of the model's topics and regex topics; the reviewer can flag a draft but never clear one; any model error, timeout or malformed JSON escalates to Wei and sends nothing.

Real mailbox (Gmail) safety design

  • Anti-spoofing: an allowlisted sender only counts if Gmail's own Authentication-Results stamp shows DKIM or DMARC pass; a forged From: is treated as unknown and costs 0 tokens.
  • Reply-To is ignored: replies go only to the authenticated sender, in the same thread (In-Reply-To/References).
  • Caps: 10 replies per sender and 50 in total per day; mailing lists, bulk mail and auto-replies are never answered.
  • Dry-run by default: nothing is transmitted until MAILBOX_SEND=on.
  • Old-mail cutoff: only mail that arrives after the mailbox is first connected is handled. The first live test found about 2,360 old unread emails in a real inbox; they are left untouched.
  • No public dashboard: the app refuses to listen on a public interface without authentication. The Fly.io deployment (decision D7) has no public port; Wei reaches the dashboard through fly proxy.

Two product decisions with safety trade-offs

D5: every proposed change waits for Wei, even when the spec settles it. A spec line can be outdated, and changes are where expensive mistakes happen. Cost: suppliers wait a few hours longer on change questions. D6: holding replies auto-send at Medium. At night a supplier hears "Wei will review the pricing and reply directly" within seconds. The reply must pass every compliance check (no numbers, no commitment verbs) and is never sent if any warning fired. Cost: a supplier may read a quick holding reply as a coming "yes".

The honest finding. In this late-stage thread only 1 of 43 emails was safe to answer fully on its own. The value showed up instead as triage (17 emails needing no reply), 13 ready-to-approve drafts, and fast holding replies. The hypothesis that 40–60% of supplier emails during DFM are auto-answerable (Wei's estimate) is not yet supported; it needs early-stage DFM threads, where technical questions dominate.

8. Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • Live outcomes vary. Email 2 (a PCBA clarification) escalated in 2 of 3 live runs because the manager read a timing question into it; the demo script expects "needs approval". The variation always goes toward the stricter outcome, but it shows the model is not deterministic.
  • Auto-answer share is unproven (§7): 1 of 43 on one real, late-stage thread.
  • English only; inbound attachments (drawings, photos) are never read, by design.
  • Regex detectors have false positives (conservative on purpose) and could miss novel phrasing; the outbound checks are the second layer.
  • Not hosted yet. The Fly.io deployment is prepared (Dockerfile, private-network-only config, D7) but not deployed, and Docker was not available to test the image build. Graders run it locally from a fresh clone.
  • Persona trade-off. Replies are signed "John"; if a supplier learns it's an agent, trust may suffer. The agent never claims to be human, and identity questions always go to Wei.
  • The sample product, company and suppliers are fictional.

What I'd build next

  1. An eval set from 50 real, anonymized threads: precision and recall of the commitment checks, and the true auto-answerable share. This is the moat: supplier-specific policy plus eval data.
  2. Deploy on Fly.io and run it on my own supplier threads for two weeks; measure turnaround and approvals.
  3. Bilingual drafting (English/Chinese), checked by the same deterministic layer.
  4. AgentMail as the inbox layer for multi-user inboxes; WeChat as a second channel.
  5. Integrations with spec/PLM tools so the spec stays current, which matters for D5.
