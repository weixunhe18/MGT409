# Usability Improvements

Changes that came out of my own testing and direction rather than the assignment text.
Each one records what was wrong, which side of the stack it touched, what was built,
and why it matters to a shop that is trying to sell things.

| # | Layer | What I flagged | What was added | Why it helps the business |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Frontend** | Product images looked stretched when enlarged | Removed the hardcoded `900×900` attributes that forced a square aspect ratio. The catalogue is not uniform — 10 of 102 images are rectangular (`600×900`, `720×900`, `580×614`). Detail images also never upscale, so a 450px file is shown at its true size rather than blown up soft. | The photograph is the product. A squashed garment misrepresents what the customer is buying, reads as a careless shop, and drives the returns that eat the margin on apparel. |
| 2 | **Frontend** | Handsome Dan didn't look like Handsome Dan | Redrawn against the real mascot after a web search: Olde English Bulldogge, white with a brindle patch, broad flat head, folded *rose* ears (I had floppy hound ears, which no bulldog has), heavy jowls, underbite. Drawn as SVG, so the shop ships no image it does not own. | The mascot is the brand. An officially licensed Yale store that gets its own mascot wrong undercuts the one thing it is selling — authenticity — and a drawn asset avoids a licensing problem on a licensed-merchandise site. |
| 3 | **Both** | "Sport ts" looked like one product repeated six times | They were six *different* shirts. Card labels truncated with an ellipsis, and catalogue names share long prefixes — "Tri Blend Sports **Baseball** T Shirt" vs "… **Soccer** T Shirt" — so the distinguishing word was exactly what got cut. *Frontend:* names now wrap instead of truncating. *Backend:* the system prompt now tells the agent to write each product's full name on its own line. | A shopper who cannot tell seven products apart sees one option and leaves. The whole range was in front of them and invisible; fixing the label turned a dead end into seven things to buy. |
| 4 | **Backend** | "Do you sell any sport ts in small" returned an error | The agent searched, then checked stock on every result, blowing the tool-call limit. `search_products` gained a `size` filter and every result now carries `sizes_available`, so the question is one call. The limit was raised, and if it is ever hit the agent answers with what it already found instead of failing. | "Do you have it in my size?" is the last question before a purchase. Failing *there* is the most expensive moment to fail. Answering in one call instead of eleven also cuts the API cost per conversation. |
| 5 | **Both** | No limit on repeated login attempts, and no password reset | *Backend:* five attempts per email then a 15-minute lockout, tracked in a `login_throttle` table; reset links single-use, 30-minute expiry, stored only as a hash, and they clear the lockout. *Frontend:* a live countdown from the first miss, a ticking `mm:ss` lock clock, and the Forgot/Reset password pages. | Protects customer accounts from guessing, and — just as important — gives a locked-out customer a way back in. A lockout without a reset converts a forgotten password into a lost customer. |
| 6 | **Backend** | A 2-item "Performance shirt" category was too thin | Folded into T-shirt, leaving five balanced categories (29 / 27 / 27 / 11 / 8), normalized on read from 22 messy raw values. The frontend simply renders whatever categories the API reports, so no frontend change was needed. | A filter that returns two items looks broken and teaches shoppers not to trust the filters. Balanced categories make browsing feel productive instead of empty. |
| 7 | **Both** | Signed-in shoppers should see what they looked at last time | **Recent Products Viewed.** *Backend:* a new `product_views` table holding one row per shopper per product, upserted on each view, plus endpoints to record, read and clear it; members only, enforced by schema, so guests have nothing stored. The strip joins live to the catalogue and inventory, so prices and sold-out badges are current rather than cached. *Frontend:* the view is recorded on page open rather than on click — so arriving by a grid card, a chat card, or a typed URL all count — and the strip renders at the top of the storeroom with a **Clear** link. | Most people do not buy on the first visit. Showing a returning customer the three things they were weighing up removes the work of finding them again, which is one of the cheapest conversion levers in retail. It also makes having an account visibly worth something, which is what drives sign-ups. |

## Bugs found while building these

Every one of them turned out to be backend — the frontend was faithfully displaying
what it was given.

| Layer | Where | What went wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **Backend** | Recently viewed | `datetime('now')` has only second resolution, and a shopper can open several products inside one second. Re-viewing a product bumped its count but left it in position, because every row shared a timestamp. Now stored with millisecond precision, so "most recent" means it. |
| **Backend** | Search | "What hoodies do you have?" answered *"We don't have any hoodies"* — with 27 in the catalogue. Matching is substring-based and `"hoodies"` is not a substring of `"hoodie"`. Tokens now also try their singular forms. The agent was being perfectly honest about a search result that was itself wrong, which is the failure mode worth worrying about. |
| **Backend** | Search | "What's your cheapest fleece?" answered *"we have no fleece"* for the same reason: the first version required every token to match, and "whats" and "cheapest" appear in no product. |
